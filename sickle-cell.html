Sickle cell disease

Why this disease is important

Nigeria has more people with sickle cell disease than any other country.

Up to 30% of West Africans carry the sickle gene. It stays that common because carriers are protected against severe falciparum malaria.

When both parents are AS, each pregnancy has a one-in-four chance of an SS child.

Consider this case

A 14-month-old boy is brought to the clinic because he has cried for two days whenever he's picked up. His hands and feet are swollen, warm and tender, and he has had a fever since yesterday. His parents are both AS; they found out only after he was born.

On examination he's pale, with yellow sclerae. The tip of the spleen is palpable.

Investigations: haemoglobin 7.1 g/dL, reticulocytes raised. The blood film shows sickle cells, target cells, polychromasia and nucleated red cells. Haemoglobin electrophoresis on cellulose acetate shows a band at the S position and a faint band at F, with no Hb A. Total bilirubin is raised, mostly unconjugated; LDH is high and haptoglobin is low. A malaria film is negative and blood is sent for culture. He goes home on penicillin V, folic acid and proguanil, and the doctor talks with his parents about hydroxyurea.

Why are his hands and feet swollen and painful?

Gross morphology of sickle cell disease

Key facts

  • In Hb SS the spleen is enlarged in early childhood and a fibrous nubbin by adulthood.
  • Marrow expansion in sickle cell disease causes prominent cheekbones and a crewcut skull on X-ray.
  • In sickle cell disease, medullary infarcts kill the tips of the renal papillae: papillary necrosis.

In sickle cell disease the gross changes come from marrow expansion, infarction and chronic haemolysis. The spleen changes most with age.

Key facts

Differential diagnosis of sickle cell disease

Key facts

  • G6PD deficiency causes haemolysis in episodes after an oxidant stress, not chronic haemolysis.
  • Hereditary spherocytosis shows spherocytes and an enlarged spleen, not sickle cells and an infarcted one.

In a Nigerian child, several diseases cause haemolytic anaemia, and bone pain with fever has two causes in sickle cell disease. Each needs telling apart from sickle cell disease.

Pathogenesis of sickle cell disease

Key facts

  • Deoxygenated Hb S polymerises into fibres that distort the red cell into a sickle shape.
  • Repeated sickling lets calcium in and potassium and water out, leaving irreversibly sickled cells.
  • Sickling in sickle cell disease causes extravascular haemolysis and vaso-occlusion with infarction.

In sickle cell disease, deoxygenated Hb S polymerises and distorts the red cell. Two pathways lead from there: haemolysis and vaso-occlusion.

Pathogenesis of sickle cell disease, step by step

Step 1 of 5

Deoxygenation and polymerisation

When Hb S gives up its oxygen, its shape changes and the abnormal valine lets one molecule bind the next. Deoxygenated Hb S molecules stack into long polymer fibres, which stretch the red cell into a crescent or sickle shape.

Reversible sickling

At first the red cell regains its normal shape when it picks up oxygen again. But every sickling episode damages its membrane.

Membrane damage

Each episode lets calcium into the cell, which drives potassium and water out and damages the membrane skeleton. After many cycles the cell stays sickled even when it's carrying oxygen: an .

Haemolysis

Macrophages destroy the rigid, damaged cells. Red cells in sickle cell anaemia live about 20 days instead of 120, and the more irreversibly sickled cells in the blood, the worse the haemolysis.

Vaso-occlusion

Repeated sickling makes red cells sticky, so they adhere to endothelium. Where blood flows slowly, or inflammation slows it, sickled cells block small vessels, and the tissue beyond suffers ischaemia, pain and infarction.

Watch out. You might expect patients with the most irreversibly sickled cells to have the most crises. They don't. The number of irreversibly sickled cells sets how severe the haemolysis is, but infection, inflammation, dehydration and acidosis trigger vaso-occlusion.

Clinical features of sickle cell disease

Key facts

  • A baby with Hb SS has no symptoms until HbF falls, at about six months.
  • Hand-foot syndrome, dactylitis from small-bone infarcts, is the commonest first presentation.
  • An aplastic crisis is a sudden fall in haemoglobin and reticulocytes, from parvovirus B19 or folate lack.

Sickle cell disease shows itself as a chronic haemolytic anaemia, with pallor and jaundice, and crises on top. Symptoms start once HbF falls, at about six months.

Course of sickle cell disease

Sickle cell disease runs a very variable course: some patients have few crises, and others die in childhood. Inherited modifiers explain part of the difference, including the HbF level, the genotype and the β-globin haplotype.

Complications of sickle cell disease

Key facts

  • Acute chest syndrome is the commonest cause of death in children and adults with sickle cell disease.
  • Vaso-occlusion causes avascular necrosis, priapism, retinopathy and renal papillary necrosis; stroke comes from stenosis of large cerebral arteries.
  • Leg ulcers in sickle cell disease come from vascular stasis and local ischaemia, and are slow to heal.

In sickle cell disease, vaso-occlusion can infarct almost any organ, and chronic haemolysis adds complications of its own.

Sickle cell trait

Sickle cell trait (AS) is the carrier state: one sickle gene and one normal β-globin gene. Families often confuse it with sickle cell disease.

Functional asplenia in sickle cell disease

Key facts

  • Children and adults with sickle cell disease are functionally asplenic, so encapsulated bacteria aren't cleared.
  • A child's enlarged spleen is congested with sickled cells, so it can't trap and kill bacteria.
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae can cause rapid, fulminant sepsis in a patient with sickle cell disease.

In sickle cell disease the spleen stops clearing bacteria long before it disappears, which exposes the patient to encapsulated bacteria.

Connect this to other specialties
The spleen on the blood film

in sickle cell disease is loss of splenic function: the spleen is present but no longer clears bacteria from the blood. In childhood trapped sickled cells congest the spleen; by adulthood repeated infarcts have shrunk it to a small fibrous remnant.

Functional asplenia and pneumococcal sepsis in sickle cell disease

  1. Streptococcus pneumoniae has a polysaccharide capsule that resists phagocytosis, and so do Haemophilus influenzae type b and Neisseria meningitidis. Phagocytes take up these once antibody coats them: .

  2. In a healthy spleen, macrophages in the red pulp filter the blood and ingest opsonised bacteria. B cells in the marginal zone make IgM against bacterial capsules without help from T cells.

  3. In early childhood sickled cells are trapped in the red pulp. The spleen is enlarged, but congestion stops it trapping and killing bacteria, so even a child with a big spleen can die of septicaemia.

  4. Stasis in the red pulp causes hypoxic damage and repeated infarcts. By adulthood the spleen is a small fibrous remnant: autosplenectomy.

  5. Without a working spleen, encapsulated bacteria multiply in the blood. Pneumococcal infection can cause rapid, fulminant sepsis and meningitis, and Haemophilus infection is the next most frequent in childhood.

Watch out. You might expect a big spleen to protect a child against infection. In sickle cell disease it doesn't: trapped sickled cells congest the enlarged spleen, so a child with a palpable spleen is still at risk of fatal septicaemia.

Genetic counselling and antenatal diagnosis of sickle cell disease, step by step

  1. A pregnant woman's test shows an abnormal haemoglobin, such as AS. Her partner is then tested to find out whether he carries one too.

  2. When both partners carry an abnormal haemoglobin, the couple is at risk of a child with sickle cell disease. They're given genetic counselling and offered antenatal diagnosis.

  3. Most samples come from a chorionic villus biopsy; cells from amniotic fluid are sometimes used. Either sample gives fetal DNA to test.

  4. The fetal β-globin gene is amplified by the polymerase chain reaction and tested for the sickle mutation. A fetus with one normal and one sickle gene is AS; a fetus with two sickle genes is SS.

Key facts

  • In splenic sequestration an infant's spleen enlarges suddenly while the haemoglobin falls.

Bilirubin metabolism in sickle cell haemolysis, step by step

Step 1 of 5

Red cells destroyed early

Red cells in sickle cell anaemia live about 20 days, a sixth of the normal lifespan. Phagocytes break most of them down outside the vessels: extravascular haemolysis. Globin returns to the body's protein pool, and iron is taken out of the haem and reused.

Haem becomes bilirubin

Once its iron is removed, haem is converted to bilirubin. This bilirubin hasn't passed through the liver yet, so it's . In sickle cell disease it's made faster than the liver can excrete it, so the plasma level rises and the eyes turn yellow.

Carried on albumin

Unconjugated bilirubin isn't water-soluble, so it travels in the plasma bound to albumin. Bound to albumin, it can't be excreted in the urine. The patient is jaundiced, but the urine dipstick shows no bilirubin: .

Conjugated in the liver

Hepatocytes take up the bilirubin and join it to glucuronate, a reaction catalysed by UDP-glucuronyl transferase. Conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble and is secreted into bile. In haemolysis much more of it than normal reaches the gut.

Urobilinogen in the urine

Gut bacteria turn bilirubin into stercobilinogen, also called . Some is absorbed and sent back into bile by the liver. In heavy haemolysis more can be absorbed than the liver can send back, and the extra passes in the urine, so urine urobilinogen rises.

Watch out. Yellow eyes in sickle cell disease are easy to read as liver disease. But a healthy liver can't keep up when bilirubin is made faster than it can be excreted. That's prehepatic jaundice, and the bilirubin is unconjugated.

Key facts

Mechanism of hydroxyurea in sickle cell disease

  1. Hydroxyurea inhibits . Dividing cells run short of the deoxynucleotides they need to copy DNA and stop in the S phase. This is how it acts against myeloid leukaemia.

  2. In sickle cell disease the same drug raises the production of fetal haemoglobin (HbF) in red cell precursors. The pathway from enzyme block to more HbF isn't fully understood.

  3. HbF in each red cell interferes with the polymerisation of deoxygenated Hb S. Red cells sickle less, so fewer of them block small vessels.

  4. Hydroxyurea lowers the white cell count, which damps inflammation. Red cells grow larger, so the Hb S inside them is less concentrated. The drug is also metabolised to nitric oxide, which dilates vessels and inhibits platelet aggregation.

  5. Painful crises become fewer in children and adults, and the anaemia lessens. In children with Hb SS in sub-Saharan Africa, hydroxyurea also reduces malaria infections.

Folic acid in sickle cell disease

Key facts

  • Folic acid by mouth is part of steady-state care for every patient with sickle cell disease.
  • Making thymidine for DNA uses up tetrahydrofolate, so a dividing marrow needs a steady folate supply.
  • Folate deficiency in sickle cell disease causes an aplastic crisis, with megaloblastic change in the marrow.

Every patient with sickle cell disease takes folic acid in steady state. Haemolysis keeps the marrow dividing fast, and a dividing marrow uses up folate.

Connect this to other specialties

Folate depletion in sickle cell disease, step by step

  1. Sickle red cells are destroyed long before the end of a normal red cell lifespan. The marrow makes red cells much faster to keep the haemoglobin up.

  2. Every new red cell precursor copies its DNA. Making thymidine (dTMP) for DNA uses up one molecule of tetrahydrofolate each time, and must keep regenerating it.

  3. Excess cell turnover raises the body's need for folate, so haemolytic anaemias are a cause of folate deficiency, especially when the diet is poor.

  4. Without enough folate, precursors can't complete DNA synthesis and become megaloblastic. Red cell production falls, and the patient has an aplastic crisis.

  5. Folic acid by mouth replaces the folate the marrow uses. It's part of steady-state care for every patient.

Newer drugs act on single steps in sickling or in vaso-occlusion. Only stem cell transplant and gene therapy can cure the disease.

Pot station
You're handed a pot holding the spleen of an adult with Hb SS. It's about 5 cm long, light and firm, grey-brown, with gritty, scarred areas on the cut surface. Describe it, then name the process.

A small, shrunken, firm spleen with fibrous scarring and gritty, calcified areas on the cut surface. In an adult with Hb SS this is autosplenectomy: a spleen reduced to a fibrous nubbin by repeated infarcts.

What did this spleen look like in early childhood?

Moderately enlarged, dark red and congested, with sickled cells trapped in the red pulp.

Why did it shrink?

Blood moves slowly through the red pulp, so the cells sickle there. Chronic stasis causes hypoxic damage and repeated infarcts, and each infarct heals by fibrosis.

What would you expect down the microscope?

Fibrosis, iron pigment and calcification, with siderofibrotic nodules beside vessels packed with sickled cells.

What does the loss of this spleen mean for the patient?

Functional asplenia. Encapsulated bacteria, above all Streptococcus pneumoniae, aren't cleared from the blood, and sepsis can be fulminant. Howell-Jolly bodies appear on the blood film.

Pot station
You're handed a femoral head from a 35-year-old man with Hb SS, cut through its centre. Under the joint surface is a pale yellow, wedge-shaped segment, and the surface above it has collapsed and flattened. Describe it, then name the lesion.

A femoral head with a pale, wedge-shaped segment of dead bone under the articular surface, and collapse and flattening of the surface above it: avascular necrosis.

What caused it in a patient with sickle cell disease?

Vaso-occlusion. Marrow has sluggish blood flow and a high metabolic rate, so sickled cells block its vessels and the bone under the joint infarcts.

What would the dead bone show down the microscope?

Dead trabeculae with empty lacunae and no osteocytes. Osteoclasts resorb the dead bone, and new bone is laid on the living trabeculae.

What does the patient go on to develop?

Hip pain, and later osteoarthritis of the hip as the flattened head wears the joint.

A child with sickle cell disease has fever and a painful bone. What else must you tell apart from an infarct, and how?

Osteomyelitis, most often from Salmonella. The two look alike at the bedside and on X-ray. Blood and pus or bone are cultured, and the bone in osteomyelitis shows neutrophils and a sequestrum.

Slide station
A blood film from a 10-year-old girl. Many red cells are crescent-shaped, some look like targets, and some larger red cells stain blue-grey. Name the three findings and the disease.

Sickle cells, target cells and polychromasia: the film of sickle cell anaemia.

What does the polychromasia tell you?

Young red cells released early by a marrow replacing cells that live about 20 days. The reticulocyte count is raised.

Her haemoglobin is 7 g/dL and she's at school every day. Is that expected?

Yes. In sickle cell anaemia the haemoglobin is usually 6–9 g/dL, and the symptoms of anaemia are often mild because Hb S gives up oxygen to the tissues easily.

What would her alkaline electrophoresis show?

A band at S with faint bands at F and A2, and no Hb A. HPLC would rule out SD or S/β-thalassaemia, which can give the same strip.

Slide station
A blood film from a 19-year-old man with Hb SS. Some red cells hold a single small, round, dark purple dot. What are these?

Howell-Jolly bodies: small DNA remnants inside red cells.

Why are they there?

His spleen has stopped working. Repeated infarcts have reduced it to a fibrous remnant: autosplenectomy.

What does the same loss do to his host defences?

He's functionally asplenic, so encapsulated bacteria such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b and Neisseria meningitidis aren't cleared from the blood.

Would you see them on his sister's film? She has sickle cell trait.

No. In sickle cell trait the spleen keeps working and the blood film is normal.

Slide station
A section of spleen from a 25-year-old woman with Hb SS. The red pulp is replaced by scar, with brown pigment and calcified nodules of fibrous tissue beside vessels packed with sickled cells. Describe it, and name the nodules.

Fibrosis with iron pigment and calcification. The nodules are siderofibrotic (Gamna-Gandy) nodules: fibrous tissue holding iron and calcium, left by repeated infarcts.

Where has the iron come from?

From red cells destroyed in the spleen. Iron from their haemoglobin collects in the fibrous tissue as haemosiderin.

Is the calcification dystrophic or metastatic?

Dystrophic. It's laid down in scarred, damaged tissue while the blood calcium is normal.

Slide station
At the bench, a drop of blood has been deoxygenated on a slide. Down the microscope many red cells have turned into sickles. Name the test, and say what a positive result shows.

The sickling test. A reducing agent such as sodium metabisulphite deoxygenates the blood under a coverslip, and red cells holding Hb S sickle, so a positive test shows Hb S is present.

The blood came from a well 30-year-old man whose haemoglobin is normal. Does the result mean sickle cell disease?

No. A carrier's red cells sickle on the slide too, so the test is positive in AS as well as SS. A well man with a normal haemoglobin most likely has sickle cell trait.

Which test names his genotype?

Haemoglobin electrophoresis, the most widely available genotype test in Nigeria, or HPLC. In the trait Hb S is 25–45% of the total, so the Hb A band is the larger.

Which other test shows only whether Hb S is present?

The solubility test. Deoxygenated Hb S is insoluble in a dithionite and phosphate solution, which turns cloudy in AS and SS alike.

Viva station
At the bench you're shown a haemoglobin electrophoresis strip. Tell me how you read it.

On cellulose acetate at alkaline pH each haemoglobin runs its own distance from the origin. Hb A runs furthest, then F, then S, with C and A2 nearest the origin. Which bands are there, and which is larger, gives the genotype.

Her lane shows bands at A and S, the A band larger. What's her genotype, and what does it mean for her?

AS, sickle cell trait. Hb S is 25–45% of the total, so Hb A is the larger band. It's a carrier state: no anaemia, a normal blood film and a normal life expectancy.

Her fiancé's lane shows a single band at S, with faint bands at F and A2. What do you tell the couple?

He most likely has sickle cell anaemia, though HPLC should rule out SD and S/β-thalassaemia, which give the same strip. With AS and SS parents, each pregnancy has a two-in-four chance of SS and a two-in-four chance of AS.

Viva station
Tell me what you know about sickle cell disease.

A group of inherited haemoglobinopathies caused by the sickle β-globin gene, inherited from both parents (SS) or with another abnormal β-globin gene (SC, S/β-thalassaemia). Deoxygenated Hb S polymerises, the red cells sickle, and the result is chronic haemolysis and vaso-occlusion with infarcts.

Why do the red cells sickle?

Valine replaces glutamic acid at position 6 of β-globin. When Hb S gives up its oxygen, the valine lets the molecules bind each other and stack into polymer fibres that distort the cell. How far it goes depends on the HbF and Hb A in the cell, its Hb S concentration and its transit time through the capillary.

Why does a patient with sickle cell disease die of pneumococcal sepsis when a patient with the trait doesn't?

In sickle cell disease the spleen is congested with sickled cells in childhood and infarcted away by adulthood, so encapsulated bacteria aren't cleared from the blood. In the trait Hb A is more than half the haemoglobin and slows polymerisation, so red cells rarely sickle in the body and the spleen keeps working.

Viva questions

18 topics

Morbid Anatomy

Tell me what you know about sickle cell disease.

Sickle cell disease is a group of inherited haemoglobin disorders caused by the sickle β-globin gene, inherited from both parents or with another abnormal β-globin gene such as Hb C. Deoxygenated Hb S polymerises, the red cells sickle, and the result is chronic haemolytic anaemia and vaso-occlusion.

  1. Why do the red cells sickle?

    Valine replaces glutamic acid at position 6 of β-globin. When Hb S gives up its oxygen, the valine lets the molecules bind each other and stack into long polymers that distort the cell.

  2. Where are most of the red cells destroyed in sickle cell disease?

    Outside the vessels, by macrophages: in the spleen in childhood, and in the liver and marrow once the spleen has infarcted away.

Tell me what you know about the spleen in sickle cell disease.

The spleen is enlarged and congested in early childhood, with sickled cells trapped in the red pulp. Chronic stasis causes hypoxic damage and infarcts, and repeated infarcts shrink it to a small fibrous nubbin by adulthood: autosplenectomy.

  1. What would you see down the microscope in the adult spleen?

    Fibrosis, iron pigment and calcification, with siderofibrotic nodules beside vessels full of sickled cells.

  2. What makes the cut surface of the adult spleen gritty?

    Calcified siderofibrotic nodules: fibrous tissue holding iron and calcium, left by repeated infarcts.

Tell me about the crises of sickle cell disease.

Crises are vaso-occlusive, aplastic or haemolytic. Vaso-occlusive crises are painful, from bone infarcts, or visceral, with sickling and pooling of blood in the lung, spleen or liver. Painful crises are the commonest.

  1. What causes an aplastic crisis?

    Parvovirus B19 infection or folate deficiency stops red cell production. Red cells live only about 20 days, so the haemoglobin and the reticulocytes fall fast.

  2. What is usually the first presentation in a young child?

    Hand-foot syndrome: painful dactylitis from infarcts of the small bones of the hands and feet, usually before the age of five.

Tell me what you know about the complications of sickle cell disease.

Most come from vaso-occlusion: acute chest syndrome, stroke and silent cerebral infarcts, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, priapism, proliferative retinopathy and renal papillary necrosis. Chronic haemolysis causes pigment gallstones. Leg ulcers and pulmonary hypertension are common too.

  1. What happens to the femoral head?

    Avascular necrosis. A wedge of bone under the joint infarcts and slowly collapses, flattening the femoral head and leading to osteoarthritis.

  2. What causes priapism in sickle cell disease, and what does it lead to?

    Vaso-occlusion in the penis causes a painful, sustained erection. Repeated episodes cause fibrosis and erectile dysfunction.

Tell me what you know about sickle cell trait.

Sickle cell trait is the carrier state, AS. There's no anaemia, the blood film is normal and life expectancy is normal. Haematuria from small infarcts of the renal papillae is the commonest symptom.

  1. Why do AS red cells rarely sickle in the body?

    Hb A makes up more than half of the haemoglobin, and Hb A interacts only weakly with Hb S, so it slows polymerisation.

  2. When can AS red cells sickle?

    With severe deoxygenation, so care is needed in anaesthesia, pregnancy and at high altitude.

Microbiology and Parasitology

Tell me why a child with sickle cell disease is at risk of pneumococcal sepsis.

Children and adults with sickle cell disease are functionally asplenic. In early childhood trapped sickled cells congest the spleen so it can't clear bacteria; later infarcts destroy it. Encapsulated bacteria like pneumococcus then go uncleared.

  1. His spleen is palpable. Doesn't that mean it's working?

    No. The enlarged spleen is congested with trapped sickled cells, which stops it trapping and killing bacteria.

  2. Why do encapsulated bacteria need the spleen to clear them?

    The capsule resists phagocytosis. Bacteria must be opsonised with antibody before splenic macrophages ingest them, and marginal zone B cells make IgM against capsules without T cell help.

  3. Which encapsulated organisms do you worry about?

    Streptococcus pneumoniae above all, then Haemophilus influenzae type b and Neisseria meningitidis.

Tell me what you know about osteomyelitis in sickle cell disease.

Bacteria seed bone already damaged by infarcts. Salmonella is the usual cause, then other Gram-negative rods such as Escherichia coli.

  1. Why Salmonella?

    A working spleen clears opsonised bacteria from the blood, and this patient has no working spleen. Salmonella bacteraemia then persists and settles in bone damaged by infarcts.

  2. A child has fever and a painful swollen limb. How do you tell infection from an infarct?

    Not reliably at the bedside or on X-ray: the two look alike. Blood is cultured, and pus or bone from the site is cultured to confirm infection.

Tell me what you know about the aplastic crisis in sickle cell disease.

An aplastic crisis is a sudden fall in haemoglobin with reticulocytes almost absent. Parvovirus B19 usually causes it by infecting and killing erythroblasts, and red cells that live so short a time can't carry the patient through even a few days without production.

  1. How do you confirm it?

    Reticulocytes are almost absent on the count, and IgM antibody to parvovirus B19 rises, followed by IgG.

  2. What kind of virus is parvovirus B19?

    A small, non-enveloped virus with single-stranded DNA.

  3. What else can cause an aplastic crisis?

    Folate deficiency.

Haematology and Immunology

Tell me what you know about the laboratory diagnosis of sickle cell disease.

The sickling and solubility tests show that Hb S is present, but they can't tell AS from SS. The genotype comes from haemoglobin electrophoresis, HPLC or a point-of-care test. In Hb SS no Hb A is found, and the film shows sickle cells and target cells.

  1. Why might alkaline electrophoresis call an SD patient SS?

    Hb D runs at the same place as Hb S on cellulose acetate at alkaline pH. HPLC separates the two.

  2. A strip shows bands at A and S, with more S than A. Is that sickle cell trait?

    No. In the trait Hb S is 25 to 45% of the total, so Hb A is the larger band. More S than A suggests Hb S/β⁺-thalassaemia, or an SS patient who's been transfused.

Tell me what you know about fetal haemoglobin in sickle cell disease.

HbF is α2γ2. It interacts only weakly with Hb S and stays out of the polymer, so red cells rich in HbF resist sickling. A baby with Hb SS has no symptoms until HbF falls at about six months, and a patient with a high HbF has milder disease.

  1. How is HbF measured, and what level is usual in Hb SS?

    By HPLC, which gives the share of each haemoglobin. In Hb SS, HbF is usually 5 to 15% of the total.

  2. Apart from HbF, what decides whether a red cell sickles?

    How concentrated its Hb S is, so dehydration makes sickling worse, and how long the cell takes to cross the capillary bed, which inflammation lengthens.

Tell me how sickle cell disease is inherited.

It's autosomal recessive. A child with sickle cell anaemia has inherited a sickle gene from each parent. When both parents are AS, each pregnancy has a one-in-four chance of SS, a one-in-two chance of AS and a one-in-four chance of AA.

  1. How would you diagnose sickle cell anaemia before birth?

    Fetal DNA from a chorionic villus biopsy or amniotic fluid is amplified by PCR and tested for the sickle mutation.

Tell me what you know about splenic sequestration.

In splenic sequestration, sickled cells pool suddenly in an infant's spleen. The spleen enlarges, the haemoglobin falls and the child has abdominal pain, and it tends to recur. Once the spleen has been lost, Howell-Jolly bodies appear on the film.

  1. What are Howell-Jolly bodies, and why do they appear?

    Small DNA remnants inside red cells. They appear on the film once the spleen has stopped working.

Chemical Pathology

Tell me what you know about the biochemistry of haemolysis in sickle cell disease.

Haem from destroyed red cells becomes unconjugated bilirubin, so the plasma bilirubin rises but the urine has no bilirubin. LDH and AST rise because red cells are rich in them, and haptoglobin falls as it binds free haemoglobin.

  1. Why is there no bilirubin in the urine?

    Unconjugated bilirubin isn't water-soluble and is bound to albumin, so it can't be excreted in the urine. Jaundice without bilirubin in the urine is called acholuric jaundice.

  2. Why can the urine urobilinogen rise?

    In heavy haemolysis, more bilirubin reaches the gut, where bacteria turn it into urobilinogen. When more is absorbed than the liver can send back into bile, the extra passes in the urine.

  3. Why does haptoglobin fall when the haemolysis is extravascular?

    Macrophages eating red cells release enough free haemoglobin to bind and use up the haptoglobin.

Tell me what you know about the kidney in sickle cell disease.

Sickling infarcts the renal medulla and papillae. The kidney can't concentrate urine, and the dehydration that follows makes crises more likely. Papillary infarcts cause haematuria, and chronic kidney disease is common in adults.

  1. Why can't the kidney concentrate urine?

    Concentrating urine needs the high osmolality that the loops of Henle build in the renal medulla, and ADH lets water leave the collecting ducts into it. Sickling infarcts the medulla, so the urine stays dilute.

  2. How does dilute urine make crises more likely?

    Water is lost and the patient becomes dehydrated. Red cells lose water, their Hb S becomes more concentrated, and concentrated Hb S polymerises and sickles faster.

Tell me what you know about ferritin in a patient with sickle cell disease.

Repeated transfusion adds iron the body can't excrete, so ferritin rises with iron overload. But ferritin is an acute-phase protein, and chronic haemolysis raises it too, so a high ferritin doesn't confirm overload.

  1. Where is transfused iron stored first?

    In the reticuloendothelial macrophages of the liver, spleen and bone marrow.

Pharmacology

Tell me what you know about hydroxyurea in sickle cell disease.

Hydroxyurea, or hydroxycarbamide, inhibits ribonucleotide reductase. In sickle cell disease it raises fetal haemoglobin, which interferes with Hb S polymerisation, so painful crises are fewer. Its main adverse effects are marrow suppression, gastrointestinal upset and teratogenicity.

  1. Apart from raising HbF, how else does it help?

    It lowers the white cell count, which damps inflammation. It makes red cells larger, so the Hb S inside them is less concentrated. And it's metabolised to nitric oxide, a vasodilator that inhibits platelet aggregation.

  2. Which blood count do you watch while a patient takes it, and why?

    The neutrophil count, because hydroxyurea suppresses the marrow and can cause severe neutropenia.

Tell me why a child with sickle cell disease takes penicillin every day.

From early childhood the spleen doesn't work: sickled cells congest it, then infarcts destroy it. Without it, encapsulated bacteria such as the pneumococcus cause fulminant sepsis. Daily oral penicillin V prevents pneumococcal infection, alongside vaccines against pneumococcus, Haemophilus and meningococcus.

  1. How does penicillin kill the bacterium?

    It binds penicillin-binding proteins and blocks the cross-linking of peptidoglycan, so a growing bacterium can't build its cell wall and dies.

  2. What's its main adverse effect?

    Hypersensitivity, from a rash to serum sickness and, very rarely, anaphylaxis.

Tell me about the analgesics used in a painful sickle cell crisis.

Pain is treated by severity: paracetamol or ibuprofen for mild pain, and diclofenac or an opioid such as pentazocine or morphine for moderate to severe pain. NSAIDs can injure a kidney already damaged by sickling. Opioids depress breathing, and pethidine's metabolite norpethidine can cause seizures.

  1. Why does an NSAID harm a kidney damaged by sickling?

    Renal blood flow is held steady by prostaglandins, and NSAIDs block their synthesis. Sickling has often already damaged the kidney, with papillary necrosis and falling function.

    Haematology and Immunology
    Red cells in sickle cell anaemia live about 20 days, a sixth of normal. The marrow makes more red cells to keep up, so the reticulocyte count is high and the film shows polychromasia.

    Acholuric jaundice
    Jaundice with no bilirubin in the urine, because the extra bilirubin is unconjugated and bound to albumin. Haemolysis is a cause.
    Red cell alloimmunisation
    The formation of antibodies against antigens on transfused red cells that the patient's own red cells lack.
    Anion gap
    Sodium plus potassium, minus chloride and bicarbonate, in the plasma. It stands for the anions that aren't measured, and it widens when an acid such as lactate replaces bicarbonate.
    Apoptosis
    Regulated cell death: the cell switches on enzymes that dismantle it from within, in pieces that are eaten without inflammation.
    Atrophy
    A decrease in the size of an organ or tissue, from smaller cells, fewer cells, or both.
    Autosplenectomy
    Loss of the spleen to repeated infarcts, which shrink it to a small nubbin of fibrous tissue. In sickle cell disease it's complete by adulthood.
    Avascular necrosis (osteonecrosis)
    Infarction of bone and marrow. Under a joint, a wedge of dead bone forms and can collapse, as in the femoral head.
    Blackwater fever
    Sudden intravascular haemolysis in falciparum malaria, with haemoglobin in the urine turning it dark, and acute kidney injury.
    Blood schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the asexual parasites inside red cells, the stage that causes the illness. Every drug that treats malaria is one.
    C-peptide
    A peptide the pancreas releases along with insulin. It's raised when the patient's own pancreas makes too much insulin, and suppressed when insulin is injected.
    Caseous necrosis (caseation)
    Necrosis in which dead tissue loses all structure and becomes soft, granular and cheese-like. Down the microscope it has no nuclei and no preserved architecture.
    Caspase
    One of a family of enzymes that carry out apoptosis.
    Cell-mediated immunity
    Defence carried out by T cells. Sensitised T cells activate macrophages so that they can kill the organisms they have phagocytosed.
    Cinchonism
    Tinnitus, headache, nausea, dizziness, flushing and blurred vision caused by quinine at treatment doses. Quinine comes from the bark of the cinchona tree.
    Coagulative necrosis
    Necrosis in which the dead cells keep their outline for some days, so the tissue's architecture can still be seen.
    Dactylitis (hand-foot syndrome)
    Painful swelling of the hands and feet caused by infarcts of their small bones. It's often the first presentation of sickle cell disease.
    Dihydrofolate reductase
    The enzyme that reduces dihydrofolate back to tetrahydrofolate, the active form of folate used to make DNA. Proguanil blocks the malaria parasite's own form of it.
    Duffy antigen (DARC)
    A chemokine receptor on the red cell surface. Plasmodium vivax uses it to enter the red cell, and most people of West African origin lack it.
    Dystrophic calcification
    Calcium deposited in dead or damaged tissue while the blood calcium is normal.
    Encapsulated bacteria
    Bacteria wrapped in a polysaccharide capsule that resists phagocytosis, such as Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b and Neisseria meningitidis. Antibody against the capsule lets phagocytes take them up.
    Endoperoxide bridge
    The bridge of two linked oxygen atoms in the artemisinin molecule. Iron is thought to split it, and the free radicals released kill the malaria parasite.
    Epithelioid macrophage
    An activated macrophage with abundant cytoplasm and an elongated nucleus. These cells form the wall of the granuloma.
    Extravascular haemolysis
    Destruction of red cells by macrophages, rather than inside the blood vessels. It causes jaundice and pigment gallstones.
    Fat necrosis
    Death of fat cells, from released lipases or from injury, with chalky white deposits where fatty acids bind calcium.
    Ferritin
    The protein that stores iron. Its plasma level usually follows the iron stores, but it's an acute-phase protein, so inflammation raises it too.
    Fibrinoid necrosis
    Necrosis of a vessel wall where immune complexes or leaked plasma proteins are deposited, staining bright pink.
    Free radical
    A molecule with an unpaired electron, which reacts with and damages lipids, proteins and DNA.
    Functional asplenia (hyposplenism)
    A spleen that's present but can't do its work of filtering the blood and clearing bacteria. In sickle cell disease it starts in early childhood, while the spleen is still large.
    Gametocide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the sexual forms of the parasite, the gametocytes, so they can't pass the infection on to a mosquito.
    Gametocyte
    The male or female sexual form of Plasmodium in red cells. A biting mosquito takes gametocytes up, and the cycle goes on in the mosquito.
    Gangrene
    A clinical term for necrosis of a limb or part, usually from loss of blood supply.
    Granuloma
    A compact collection of epithelioid macrophages, often with multinucleate giant cells, ringed by lymphocytes.
    Haemoglobinopathy
    An inherited disorder caused by a mutation that changes the structure of a globin chain, such as sickle haemoglobin (Hb S) or Hb C.
    Haemosiderin
    A golden-brown pigment of stored iron, seen where red cells have broken down or iron is in excess.
    Haemozoin (malarial pigment)
    A brown-black pigment the parasite leaves from the haemoglobin it digests. Ruptured red cells release it, and macrophages in the spleen, liver and marrow take it up.
    Haptoglobin
    A plasma protein that binds free haemoglobin. The pair is removed from the circulation, so the haptoglobin level falls in haemolysis.
    Haemoglobin electrophoresis
    A test that moves haemoglobins along a strip in an electric field, so each kind shows as a band at its own position.
    Hepcidin
    A hormone that keeps iron inside macrophages and blocks its absorption from the gut. Inflammation raises it, which starves the marrow of iron.
    Howell-Jolly body
    A small remnant of DNA inside a red cell. Howell-Jolly bodies show on the blood film when the spleen has stopped working.
    High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)
    A test that passes haemoglobin through a column. Each kind leaves at its own time, so the machine names it and measures its share of the total.
    HRP2 (histidine-rich protein 2)
    A protein made by P. falciparum and no other species, and the antigen most malaria rapid diagnostic tests detect.
    Hyperplasia
    An increase in the number of cells in a tissue, in cells that can divide.
    Hypersplenism
    An enlarged spleen with a fall in at least one blood cell line while the bone marrow works normally.
    Hypertrophy
    An increase in the size of cells, and so of the organ, without more cells.
    Hypnozoite
    A dormant liver form of P. vivax and P. ovale. Hypnozoites can wake weeks or years later and cause a relapse.
    Hypoxia
    Too little oxygen reaching the cells.
    Infarct
    An area of ischaemic necrosis caused by loss of the blood supply, in sickle cell disease usually from vessels blocked by sickled cells.
    Intermittent preventive therapy
    Treatment doses of an antimalarial given at set times to people at high risk, such as pregnant women, whether or not they're infected.
    Iron chelation
    Treatment with a drug that binds iron tightly so it can leave the body in urine or faeces. It treats the iron overload of repeated blood transfusion.
    Irreversibly sickled cell
    A red cell whose membrane has been damaged by many cycles of sickling, so it stays sickled even when it's carrying oxygen.
    Ischaemia
    Too little blood reaching a tissue: less oxygen, and less of everything else the blood brings and takes away.
    Karyolysis
    A nucleus fading away as its DNA is broken down.
    Karyorrhexis
    A pyknotic nucleus breaking into fragments.
    Lactic acidosis
    A high anion gap metabolic acidosis from lactate building up in the blood. Its commonest cause is tissue short of oxygen, from poor perfusion, hypoxia or severe anaemia.
    Langhans giant cell
    A multinucleate cell formed when macrophages fuse. Its nuclei sit in a horseshoe around the edge of the cell.
    Lipofuscin
    A yellow-brown 'wear and tear' pigment of oxidised lipid, building up in long-lived cells with age.
    Liquefactive necrosis
    Necrosis in which enzymes digest the dead tissue into a liquid.
    Merozoite
    The form of Plasmodium released when an infected liver cell or red cell bursts. Each merozoite invades a red cell.
    Metaplasia
    The replacement of one mature cell type by another, better able to stand a particular stress.
    Metastatic calcification
    Calcium deposited in normal tissue because the blood calcium is high.
    Methaemoglobinaemia
    Haemoglobin in the blood with its iron oxidised from the Fe2+ to the Fe3+ state. Drugs such as primaquine can cause it, and the patient looks cyanosed.
    Necrosis
    Death of cells in a living tissue, with their membranes breaking and their contents leaking out, which brings in inflammation.
    Necrotising granuloma
    A granuloma whose centre has died. In tuberculosis the dead centre is caseous.
    Opsonisation
    Coating of a microbe with antibody or complement so phagocytes can recognise and ingest it.
    Osteomyelitis
    Infection of bone and its marrow. In sickle cell disease bacteria seed bone already damaged by infarction.
    P-selectin
    An adhesion molecule that helps sickle cells stick to the walls of blood vessels, one step in vaso-occlusion.
    Pancytopenia
    A fall in all three blood cell lines: red cells, white cells and platelets.
    Paraprotein
    One immunoglobulin made in excess by a single clone of B cells. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a narrow, dense band, usually in the gamma region.
    Parasitaemia
    Malaria parasites in the circulating blood, counted on a film as the share of red cells infected or the number per microlitre.
    Penicillin-binding protein (PBP)
    A bacterial enzyme that cross-links the peptide chains of peptidoglycan, which gives the cell wall its strength. Beta-lactam antibiotics bind its active site and block it.
    Pigment gallstone
    A gallstone made of calcium bilirubinate. It forms when bile carries a high load of unconjugated bilirubin, as in chronic haemolysis.
    pLDH (Plasmodium lactate dehydrogenase)
    An enzyme of the malaria parasite, detected by some rapid diagnostic tests. pLDH tests turn negative within days of cure.
    Polyclonal response
    Immunoglobulins made by many B-cell clones at once, as in chronic infection. Serum protein electrophoresis shows it as a broad, diffuse rise in the gamma region.
    Polymerisation of Hb S
    Deoxygenated sickle haemoglobin molecules binding one another into long fibres. The fibres distort the red cell into a sickle shape.
    Pyknosis
    A nucleus that has shrunk and stains dark, as its chromatin condenses.
    Radical cure
    Treatment that clears malaria parasites from both the blood and the liver, so P. vivax or P. ovale can't relapse. It needs a blood-stage drug plus primaquine or tafenoquine.
    Recrudescence
    The return of malaria from blood-stage parasites that treatment didn't clear. A relapse is different: it comes from dormant liver forms.
    Ischaemia–reperfusion injury
    Further injury to ischaemic tissue when its blood supply comes back.
    Reticulocyte
    A young red cell, just out of the marrow, that still holds RNA. The reticulocyte count shows how well the marrow is making red cells.
    Ribonucleotide reductase
    The enzyme that turns ribonucleotides into the deoxyribonucleotides a cell needs to copy its DNA. Blocking it stops dividing cells in the S phase of the cell cycle.
    Ring haemorrhage
    A small haemorrhage in the brain's white matter arranged as a ring around a small vessel, seen at autopsy in cerebral malaria.
    Schizont
    A Plasmodium form whose nucleus has divided many times. It breaks up into merozoites when the liver cell or red cell bursts.
    Schüffner's dots
    Fine dots in a red cell infected with P. vivax or P. ovale, seen on a well-stained Giemsa film as the parasite matures.
    Senescence
    A permanent stop to cell division that cells reach with age.
    Sequestration
    Red cells carrying maturing P. falciparum sticking to the endothelium of small vessels, through the parasite protein PfEMP1, so they leave the circulating blood and block the capillaries.
    Siderofibrotic nodule
    A small nodule of fibrous tissue holding iron pigment and calcium, found in the scarred spleen of sickle cell disease beside vessels full of sickled cells.
    Hepatic sinusoidal lymphocytosis
    Lymphocytes filling the sinusoids of the liver on biopsy. It supports a diagnosis of tropical splenomegaly syndrome but isn't specific to it.
    Pitting
    The spleen's removal of inclusions, such as Howell-Jolly bodies and Heinz bodies, from red cells it then lets go back into the blood.
    Splenic sequestration
    Sudden pooling of sickled red cells in the spleen, which enlarges while the haemoglobin falls. Seen mostly in infants with sickle cell disease.
    Splenomegaly
    Enlargement of the spleen. A massive spleen can reach the right iliac fossa.
    Sporozoite
    The form of Plasmodium that an infected female Anopheles mosquito injects when it bites. Sporozoites travel in the blood to the liver.
    Steatosis (fatty change)
    Triglyceride building up inside cells, seen most in the liver.
    Tissue schizonticide
    An antimalarial drug that kills the parasites developing or lying dormant in the liver, such as primaquine.
    Trophozoite
    The growing stage of Plasmodium inside a red cell. On a stained film the young trophozoite looks like a ring.
    Unconjugated bilirubin
    Bilirubin that the liver hasn't yet joined to glucuronate. It isn't water-soluble, so it travels in the plasma bound to albumin and doesn't pass into the urine.
    Urobilinogen
    A colourless product that gut bacteria make from bilirubin. Some is absorbed and sent back into bile by the liver, and a little passes in the urine.
    Vaso-occlusion
    Blockage of small blood vessels by sickled red cells, which causes ischaemia, pain and infarction in the tissue they supply.
    Villous lymphocyte
    A lymphocyte with fine hair-like projections from its cytoplasm. Villous lymphocytes in the blood suggest splenic marginal zone lymphoma.
